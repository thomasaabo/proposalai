## FAR: Federal Acquisition Regulation

The FAR is the primary set of rules governing federal procurement. Every RFP references FAR clauses, and understanding them is critical to proposal compliance.

### Key FAR Parts Affecting Proposals

**FAR Part 1: Federal Acquisition Regulations System**
- Establishes that all federal procurement follows FAR rules
- Government agencies cannot waive FAR requirements

**FAR Part 2: Definitions**
- Defines standard terms: contractor, subcontractor, offeror, bid, proposal, evaluation
- "Offeror" = any company submitting a proposal; "Contractor" = the company that wins

**FAR Part 3: Improper Business Practices and Personal Conflicts of Interest**
- Prohibits conflicts of interest (e.g., you cannot have advised the government on this procurement)
- Requires disclosure of criminal history, debarment status, drug convictions
- Violation = proposal rejection or contract termination

**FAR Part 4: Administrative Matters**
- Requires SAM.gov registration and active UEI (Unique Entity Identifier)
- Contractor certifications (small business, woman-owned, etc.)
- DUNS number (legacy, being phased out in favor of UEI)

**FAR Part 5: Publicizing Contract Actions**
- Explains why RFPs are published (transparency, competition)
- Notice requirements (most RFPs published on SAM.gov 15 days before deadline)

**FAR Part 6: Competition Requirements**
- Agencies must solicit competitive bids for contracts over $25K
- "Best value" procurement is preferred (see Evaluation Methods below)
- Small business set-asides (contracts held for small business only)

**FAR Part 8: Required Sources of Supplies and Services**
- Federal supply schedules (GSA Schedule contracts)
- Many RFPs for IT services flow through existing GSA Schedules instead of open competition

**FAR Part 9: Contractor Qualifications**
- Past performance evaluation (your past work matters)
- Responsibility determination (financial stability, ethics, capacity)
- Debarment and suspension checks (convicted contractors cannot bid)

**FAR Part 10: Market Research**
- Agencies must research the market before writing RFPs
- This is why some RFPs have very specific requirements (government knew it needed X)
- Incumbent vendors are often favored by agency teams

**FAR Part 11: Describing Agency Needs**
- Defines how agencies write technical specifications
- "Specifications" vs. "Performance requirements": specs = how to build it; performance = what it must do
- Ambiguous specs = grounds for clarifying questions or protests

**FAR Part 13: Simplified Acquisition**
- Contracts under $250K can be awarded without full formal competition
- Faster process, fewer requirements, lower proposal burden
- Often called "micro-purchases" or "blanket purchase agreements" (BPAs)

**FAR Part 14: Sealed Bidding**
- "Firm fixed price" contracts, government specifies exactly what it wants
- Lowest price bid wins (no evaluation of quality, past performance, approach)
- Less common today; most procurements use negotiated (best value) awards

**FAR Part 15: Competitive Procedures** (Most Common)
- Agencies issue RFPs and evaluate proposals on multiple criteria
- Agencies can negotiate with multiple vendors before award
- "Best value" selection (see Evaluation Methods)

**FAR Part 16: Types of Contracts** (see Contract Types section)

**FAR Part 19: Small Business Programs** (see Set-Asides section)

**FAR Part 24: Protection of Privacy and Freedom of Information**
- Identifies what information in proposals is confidential vs. releasable under FOIA
- Mark sensitive data as "Proprietary" or "Confidential"

**FAR Part 30: Cost Accounting Standards**
- Contractors with federal contracts over $2M must follow CAS (Cost Accounting Standards)
- Affects how you track labor costs and overhead allocation
- Non-compliance = contract penalties or restitution

**FAR Part 31: Contract Cost Principles and Procedures**
- Defines what costs are "allowable" (you can charge to the government)
- Unallowable costs: lobbying, alcoholic beverages, entertainment, fruitless litigation
- Compliance = audit; non-compliance = recovery of unallowable costs

**FAR Part 32: Contract Financing**
- Progress payments (government pays as you perform, not after)
- Payment terms (net 30 is common; payment within 48 hours required by law)

**FAR Part 37: Service Contracting**
- Most professional services RFPs (consulting, IT, engineering, staffing)
- Requires independent government cost estimate (IGCE), kept confidential until award
- Evaluators compare your proposal cost against this secret baseline

**FAR Part 42: Contract Administration**
- Contractor performance surveillance (government monitors your work)
- Corrective action notices (formal warning for poor performance)
- Termination for cause (government can cancel contract and award to another vendor)

### FAR Compliance Checklist for Proposals

- [ ] Is your company registered in SAM.gov with an active UEI?
- [ ] Have you disclosed any conflicts of interest or debarment status?
- [ ] Are you eligible for the set-aside category (if any)?
- [ ] Have you included all mandatory certifications (small business, woman-owned, etc.)?
- [ ] Have you addressed every evaluation criterion stated in the RFP?
- [ ] Have you followed the page limits, formatting, and submission format exactly?
- [ ] Have you provided evidence of past performance on similar work?
- [ ] Have you certified your cost estimate is accurate and complies with CAS/FAR Part 31?
- [ ] Have you marked proprietary information clearly?
- [ ] Have you read and acknowledged all FAR clauses listed in the RFP?

## DFARS: Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement

DFARS extends FAR for Department of Defense (DoD) procurements. If your RFP is issued by DoD, Army, Navy, Air Force, Marines, or DIA, DFARS applies.

### Key DFARS Requirements

**DFARS Part 202: Definitions**
- Adds defense-specific terminology (military departments, defense industrial base, cybersecurity maturity model certification)

**DFARS Part 204: Administrative Matters**
- Requires FAR compliance plus additional DoD-specific items
- Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA) oversight

**DFARS Part 207: Acquisition Planning**
- DoD must publish RFPs on sam.gov AND beta.sam.gov
- Timeline: 30 days for full and open competition (standard)
- Accelerated timeline: 15 days if justified

**DFARS Part 209: Contractor Qualifications**
- Federal Awardee Performance and Integrity Information System (FAPIIS) review
- Contractor past performance is mandatory (not optional)
- Unresolved audit findings = automatic bid rejection

**DFARS Part 215: Competitive Procedures** (DoD-Specific Negotiation)
- "Defense competitive procedure" = standard negotiated procurement
- Requires documented source selection decision (government must explain why you won/lost)

**DFARS Part 219: Small Business Programs** (DoD Small Business)
- Same small business categories as FAR 19
- DoD has additional support programs (assistance, training, contracting goals)

**DFARS Part 224: Protection of Classified Information**
- If RFP mentions "classified," "SECRET," "TOP SECRET," security clearances required
- Facility security clearance (facility SF-86 or equivalent)
- Individual security clearances (3-year background investigation standard)
- Compliance = expensive; non-compliance = proposal rejection

**DFARS Part 227: Patents, Data, and Copyrights**
- Government owns or has unlimited use rights to deliverables
- You cannot restrict what the government does with software/tools/documents you deliver
- Mark proprietary data (background information, trade secrets) as "Limited Rights"

**DFARS Part 231: Contract Cost Principles**
- Same as FAR 31, with DoD-specific additions
- R&D rates, proposal development costs, facilities capital costs
- Compliance audited heavily on DoD contracts

**DFARS Part 232: Contract Financing**
- Progress payments (typical for large DoD contracts)
- Accelerated payment for small businesses
- Cost/schedule control systems (C/SCSC) on large contracts

**DFARS Part 234: Quality Assurance**
- Government inspector at your facility
- Right to inspect facilities, records, processes
- Must maintain quality management system (ISO 9001 or equivalent)

**DFARS Part 237: Service Contracting**
- Most DoD service RFPs
- Mandatory past performance evaluation (weighted heavily)
- Security clearances required if any classified work

**DFARS Part 239: Acquisition of Information and Information Technology**
- Federal Information Security Management Act (FISMA) compliance
- NIST standards (NIST SP 800-171 for controlled unclassified information, CUI)
- System security plan (SSP) required if you will handle CUI

**DFARS Part 242: Contract Administration**
- Cost/schedule control systems (CSCS) on contracts over $50M
- Earned value management (EVM) mandatory on large contracts
- Government audit rights (DCAA - Defense Contract Audit Agency)
- Termination for convenience allowed; termination for cause grounds for debarment

**DFARS Part 246: Quality Assurance**
- Government receives unlimited warranty on software/deliverables
- Government can withhold payment for nonconforming work
- "Defect" = anything not meeting specification

**DFARS Part 248: Value Engineering**
- Contractors can propose cost reductions during contract
- Government gets 50% of savings; you keep 50%
- Creates incentive for efficiency

### DFARS Compliance: Security, Cybersecurity, Supply Chain

**Facility Security Clearance (FSC)**
- If RFP involves classified information, company must hold FSC
- Facility Security Officer (FSO) required
- Regular inspections by DCSA

**Individual Security Clearances**
- SECRET clearance: 3-year investigation, ~$3K-8K per person
- TOP SECRET/SCI clearance: 5-year investigation, ~$15K-20K per person
- Contractors must clear all personnel working on contract
- Wait time: 6-12 months typical (can delay project start)

**Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC)**
- DoD mandate: all contractors must achieve CMMC Level 2 minimum
- Covers NIST SP 800-171 + NIST SP 800-172 controls
- Third-party assessment required
- Implementation cost: $100K-500K+ depending on current state
- **Non-compliance = automatic proposal rejection**

**NIST SP 800-171 (National Institute of Standards & Technology)**
- 110 controls for protecting controlled unclassified information (CUI)
- Access controls, encryption, incident response, supply chain risk management
- Required for DoD contractors handling CUI
- Annual assessment required

**NIST SP 800-172**
- Advanced cybersecurity controls for contractors with significant CUI/classified access
- Adds 7 additional controls beyond 800-171
- Required for classified DoD contracts

**Flow-Down Requirements**
- Any security requirement in the prime contract must flow down to subcontractors
- You are responsible for your subcontractors' compliance
- Failure = breach of contract, penalties, or termination

### DFARS Compliance Checklist

- [ ] Does the RFP mention classified information? If yes, does your company have Facility Security Clearance?
- [ ] Do you have personnel with the required security clearances (SECRET, TOP SECRET)?
- [ ] Is CMMC Level 2 compliance required? Have you achieved it or committed to timeline?
- [ ] Are you implementing NIST SP 800-171 controls? (Mandatory if handling CUI)
- [ ] Have you reviewed DFARS clauses listed in RFP Section F (Contract Clauses)?
- [ ] Have you identified all flow-down requirements for subcontractors?
- [ ] Have you reviewed past performance on similar DoD contracts?
- [ ] Have you confirmed unresolved audit findings do not exist against your company?

## Evaluation Methods

### Best Value Trade-Off (Most Common)

The government will award to the proposal that provides the best overall value, balancing technical quality, management approach, past performance, and cost.

**How it works:**
1. Government establishes evaluation criteria (technical, management, past performance, cost)
2. Government assigns weights: e.g., Technical 40%, Management 20%, Past Performance 20%, Cost 20%
3. Each proposal scored 0-100 on each criterion
4. Final score = (Technical Score × 0.40) + (Management Score × 0.20) + (PP Score × 0.20) + (Cost Score × 0.20)
5. Highest score wins (all else equal)

**Cost scoring under Best Value:**
- Lowest cost proposal = 100 points
- Other proposals scored on ratio: (Lowest Cost / Your Cost) × 100
- This means if competitor bids 20% less, their cost score is much higher
- **Strategy implication**: Don't bid too high, but make sure cost is justified by your technical approach

**Past Performance under Best Value:**
- Evaluators will call your references (the companies/projects you list)
- They ask: "How well did they perform? On time? On budget? Quality?"
- A poor reference kills your chances; a great reference can win the contract
- **Strategy implication**: Only list customers who will give you glowing references

**Technical Evaluation under Best Value:**
- Evaluators look for: compliance with requirements, approach clarity, risk mitigation, technical depth
- They're looking for you to understand the problem AND have a plan to solve it
- Proof points (case studies, metrics, evidence) matter more than assertions
- **Strategy implication**: Show, don't tell

### Lowest Price Technically Acceptable (LPTA)

Government awards to the lowest-cost proposal that meets all mandatory requirements. Quality/past performance beyond the minimum standard doesn't matter.

**When government uses LPTA:**
- Commodity services (e.g., janitorial, office supplies, basic staffing)
- Highly standardized work (defined specifications, no innovation needed)
- Budget constraints (government has limited funds, wants to stretch them)

**How it works:**
1. Government defines "technically acceptable" = all mandatory requirements met
2. Evaluators mark each proposal as "acceptable" or "not acceptable"
3. Unacceptable proposals are eliminated (doesn't matter how cheap)
4. Among acceptable proposals, lowest price wins

**Strategy under LPTA:**
- Don't overspend on quality; meet minimum and bid low
- Your proposal doesn't need to be fancy; it needs to be compliant and cheap
- Weaknesses are non-negotiable (if you're "not acceptable," you're out)
- References and past performance don't matter in LPTA

### Past Performance as Primary Factor

Some RFPs make past performance the most heavily weighted factor (40-60% of score).

**Why government does this:**
- High-risk, mission-critical work where failure is costly
- New/innovative work where technical approach is secondary to team experience
- Long-standing relationship (government knows your team, trusts you)

**How it's evaluated:**
- Government contacts all references with detailed questionnaire
- Questions focus on: similar scope/size, technical performance, schedule adherence, budget management, team stability, customer satisfaction
- References are scored; your past performance score is averaged across references

**Strategy:**
- Choose references strategically: similar projects, recent (within 3 years), customers who will advocate strongly
- Brief your references on RFP requirements so they can speak to your relevant experience
- Provide metrics: dollar value, team size, schedule performance, quality measures

### Source Selection Decision

The government's source selection decision must be documented and defensible. If you lose, you can file a protest with the GAO (Government Accountability Office) arguing the evaluation was unfair.

**What makes a defensible source selection:**
- Consistent evaluation of all proposals against stated criteria
- Clear scoring rationale (not arbitrary)
- Price/performance tradeoff explained
- Past performance evaluated fairly
- No conflicts of interest or bias

**What makes a vulnerable source selection (grounds for protest):**
- Inconsistent scoring (two proposals treated differently for same issue)
- Moving targets (evaluation criteria changed mid-stream)
- Unevaluated factors (government weighted something not in the RFP)
- Bias (evaluator had conflict of interest)

## Set-Asides & Business Categories

The government prioritizes certain business types for contracting. Set-asides reserve contracts for specific categories.

### Small Business (SB)

- **Definition**: Fewer than 500 employees (varies by industry)
- **How it works**: Contract reserved for small businesses only; large businesses cannot bid
- **Percentage of federal spending**: ~23% of budget set aside for small business
- **Advantage**: Less competition (only small firms bidding); government commitment to SB success
- **Disadvantage**: You must be certified as small; if you grow past threshold, you're no longer small

### Women-Owned Small Business (WOSB)

- **Definition**: At least 51% owned, operated, managed by woman (or women)
- **Contracting program**: HUBZone certified women-owned firms get price preferences
- **Advantage**: Set-asides in WOSB category; price preference (you can bid 10% higher than competitors)
- **Certification**: Register with SBA; includes identity verification of owner

### Veteran-Owned Small Business (VOSB) & Service-Disabled VOSB (SDVOSB)

- **VOSB**: At least 51% owned by veteran
- **SDVOSB**: At least 51% owned by service-disabled veteran; also must be majority-controlled
- **Advantage**: Set-asides; price preferences
- **Evidence**: DD-214 (discharge papers), VA disability rating letter

### HUBZone Small Business

- **HUBZone**: "Historically Underutilized Business Zone"
- **Definition**: Small business owned in economically disadvantaged area; at least 35% of employees live in HUBZone
- **Advantage**: Set-asides; price preference
- **Certification**: Register with SBA with proof of location/employee residency

### 8(a) Business Development Program

- **Definition**: Socially and economically disadvantaged small business
- **Ownership**: Owned by individual(s) from disadvantaged background (minority, woman, veteran)
- **Program length**: 9 years (4 years developmental, 5 years transitional)
- **Advantage**: Access to federal contracts reserved for 8(a) firms; can bid as subcontractor on any federal contract
- **Disadvantage**: Must meet annual progress metrics or be removed from program

### Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) & Minority Institutions

- **Eligibility**: HBCU or minority-serving institution (MSI)
- **Advantage**: Set-asides for R&D contracts; federal funding for research collaborations
- **Common in**: DoD, NSF, NIH, DOE research procurements

### Large Business (Prime Contractor with Small Business Subcontractor)

- **Definition**: Large contractor subcontracts significant work to small businesses
- **Advantage**: Government gives credit for small business subcontracting
- **Requirement**: You must meet the small business subcontracting percentage you committed to or face penalties
- **Common structure**: Large prime, small business subs (often WOSB, VOSB, 8(a))

### Set-Aside Determination

When the government issues an RFP, they specify whether it's:
- **Unrestricted** = all firms can bid (large and small)
- **Small Business Set-Aside** = small businesses only
- **Women-Owned Small Business Set-Aside** = WOSB only
- **Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business Set-Aside** = SDVOSB only
- **HUBZone Set-Aside** = HUBZone small businesses only
- **8(a) Set-Aside** = 8(a) firms only

If the set-aside doesn't match your business category, you cannot bid.

## Contract Types

### Firm Fixed Price (FFP)

- **Cost structure**: You bid a price; government pays that price; you keep any profit/absorb any loss
- **Risk**: Entirely on contractor
- **Government preference**: Yes (cost is predictable)
- **When used**: Commodity services, well-defined scope, low technical risk
- **Example**: "Staff a receptionist for 2 years at $45/hour"

**Implications for proposals:**
- Your cost is firm; if you underestimate, you lose money
- Build in contingency for unknowns
- If scope changes, file a change order to increase price
- Government will hold you to price; no relief for inefficiency

### Time & Materials (T&M)

- **Cost structure**: You bill hourly labor + materials at cost; government pays for actual time spent
- **Risk**: Shared (government pays for actual work; you manage team efficiency)
- **Government preference**: Lower (cost is unpredictable; government may overpay)
- **When used**: R&D, unknown scope, high technical risk, IT services
- **Example**: "System engineering support at $150/hour + materials at cost"

**Implications for proposals:**
- Your hourly rates are fixed; profit comes from charging the right rates
- Government will monitor your hours; don't overbill
- If you're inefficient, government sees it in time tracking
- Build in overhead/G&A rates carefully (audited for reasonableness)

### Cost Plus Fixed Fee (CPFF)

- **Cost structure**: Government reimburses actual costs + you earn a fixed fee (profit)
- **Risk**: Government absorbs cost overruns; you always earn your fee
- **Government preference**: Lower (expensive; no cost control incentive)
- **When used**: R&D, exploratory work, government wants to share risk
- **Example**: "System development contract: reimburse costs + $100K fixed fee"

**Implications for proposals:**
- Your fee is guaranteed; profit = fixed fee regardless of costs
- Government incentive is to minimize your costs (reduces their spending)
- You have incentive to spend; higher costs don't reduce your profit
- Strong government oversight (audits, cost tracking)
- Less common today; government prefers T&M or FFP

### Cost Plus Award Fee (CPAF)

- **Cost structure**: Government reimburses actual costs + you earn a base fee + award fee based on performance
- **Risk**: Shared (government absorbs cost overruns; you earn fee based on execution)
- **Government preference**: Medium (incentivizes contractor performance)
- **When used**: Large programs, long-term relationships, government wants contractor motivation
- **Example**: "Cost + $500K base fee + up to $250K award fee based on schedule, quality, cost performance"

**Implications for proposals:**
- Your base fee is guaranteed; award fee is variable (0-100% earned based on performance)
- Government evaluates your performance quarterly/annually and awards fee based on metrics
- You must manage costs carefully (overruns reduce award fee potential)
- Performance metrics are subjective (government's opinion matters)
- Requires close government-contractor relationship

### Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity (IDIQ)

- **What it is**: Framework contract that allows government to order work over time, up to a stated maximum
- **Structure**: Minimum order is usually $0 (no guarantee of work); maximum is the contract ceiling
- **Duration**: Typically 1-5 years
- **Example**: "IT staffing IDIQ: up to $10M over 3 years; government orders as needed"

**How IDIQ works:**
1. Government awards contract (you're the vendor)
2. Government issues task orders (each TO is a specific assignment)
3. You perform each task order at rates agreed in IDIQ contract
4. Payment: task order amounts accumulate toward $10M ceiling
5. If ceiling is reached, contract ends

**Implications for proposals:**
- You're not bidding for a single project; you're bidding to be on a vendor list
- Your value proposition: responsiveness, flexibility, reliability
- Prove you can handle variable/uncertain workload
- IDIQ ceiling is "unlimited liability" (if you bid rates, you're locked in for all task orders)
- References should show you can deliver under IDIQ

### Blanket Purchase Agreement (BPA)

- **What it is**: Simplified version of IDIQ; much smaller maximum value
- **Typical use**: Supplies, staffing, simple services
- **Structure**: Rates are fixed; government orders as needed up to maximum
- **Example**: "Office supply BPA: $50K maximum; government orders supplies at negotiated rates"

**Implications for proposals:**
- Faster process (less formal than IDIQ)
- Lower maximum values (usually <$1M)
- Rates are what matter; service quality is secondary
- Easy to lose if you don't perform on early orders

## Compliance Requirements

### SAM.gov Registration

**What it is**: System for Award Management (SAM.gov) is the government's official database of contractors.

**Who must register**: Any company bidding on federal contracts over $25K

**What it includes**:
- Company name, address, POC
- Unique Entity Identifier (UEI) — unique company ID
- NAICS code(s) — industry classification
- Business size (small/large)
- Certifications (WOSB, VOSB, 8(a), etc.)
- Exclusions check (are you debarred?)
- Cage Code (Commercial and Government Entity code for defense contracting)

**Maintenance**:
- Registration must be renewed annually
- Must keep company info current
- Failure to maintain = proposal disqualification

**Timeline**: Registration can take 2-3 weeks; do this before bidding.

### Unique Entity Identifier (UEI)

**What it is**: 12-character unique identifier assigned by SAM.gov (replaces older DUNS number)

**Why it matters**: Government uses UEI to track all your contracts, payments, and compliance

**How to get one**: Register on SAM.gov; UEI is assigned automatically

**Who needs it**: Every contractor, every subcontractor

### NAICS Code (North American Industry Classification System)

**What it is**: Classification of your primary industry/business type

**Why it matters**: Government uses NAICS to:
- Determine if set-asides apply (small business, WOSB, etc.)
- Calculate small business subcontracting goals
- Track federal spending by industry

**Example NAICS codes**:
- 541611 = Administrative Management and General Management Consulting Services
- 541512 = Computer Systems Design Services
- 611430 = Professional and Management Development Training

**Implications**:
- Wrong NAICS code = you lose small business eligibility or get wrong category
- Choose carefully; it's used across all your proposals

### Small Business Certifications

**Self-Certification (Unverified)**:
- You claim to be small/woman-owned/VOSB/etc.
- No formal certification needed (just check boxes on SAM.gov)
- Government may challenge; if challenged, you must prove it
- Risk: Fraud claim if you misrepresent

**SBA Certification (Verified)**:
- Small Business Administration formally certifies you
- Provides official proof of status (small, 8(a), women-owned, VOSB, HUBZone)
- Stronger credibility; harder to challenge
- Cost: $0-$150 depending on certification type
- Timeline: 2-4 weeks

**Which to pursue**: If you're serious about federal work, get SBA certifications. Self-certification works in a pinch, but SBA certification is stronger.

### Security Clearances & Facility Security Clearance (FSC)

**Individual Secret Clearance**:
- Required for access to classified information (SECRET level)
- Investigation: 3 years (federal employment, credit history, references)
- Cost: Contractor typically pays ($3K-8K)
- Timeline: 6-12 months
- Valid for: 10 years

**Individual Top Secret/Sensitive Compartmented Information (TS/SCI)**:
- Required for access to highly classified information
- Investigation: 5-7 years (more extensive than Secret)
- Cost: Contractor typically pays ($15K-25K)
- Timeline: 12-24 months
- Valid for: 5 years

**Facility Security Clearance (FSC)**:
- Required if company will handle classified information at a facility
- Company-level clearance (not individual)
- Requires Facility Security Officer (FSO)
- Annual reinvestigation
- Cost: Built into contract overhead
- Process: DCSA (Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency) review

**Implications**:
- If RFP mentions "Secret," "classified," or "CUI," clearances are likely required
- Clearing personnel takes time; start early
- Cost is significant; factor into proposal pricing
- Family members may be interviewed (security concern = spouse's criminal history, foreign relatives)
- Don't promise clearances you don't have or can't get

### CMMC (Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification)

**What it is**: DoD mandate for all contractors; certification of cybersecurity capabilities

**Levels**:
- CMMC Level 1: Basic cyber hygiene (14 practices)
- CMMC Level 2: Intermediate cybersecurity (23 practices, includes NIST SP 800-171)
- CMMC Level 3: Advanced (110 practices, includes NIST SP 800-171, NIST SP 800-172)

**Current requirements** (as of 2026):
- DoD FARs require CMMC Level 2 for contracts handling CUI (controlled unclassified information)
- CMMC Level 3 only for classified contracts

**Assessment**:
- Third-party assessor certifies your compliance
- Certification valid for 3 years
- Cost: $20K-100K+ depending on current state and company size

**Implications**:
- Non-compliance = automatic proposal rejection on DoD contracts
- Implementation takes 6-12 months
- Must maintain compliance (ongoing cost)
- Subcontractors must also be CMMC-compliant

### Section 508 Accessibility (IT Contracts)

**What it is**: Requirement that all IT deliverables are accessible to people with disabilities

**Applies to**:
- Websites, web applications, software
- Documents (PDFs, Word files must be accessible)
- Videos (captions required)

**Standards**: WCAG 2.1 Level AA (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines)

**Implications for proposals**:
- If RFP includes software/web/documents, you must commit to 508 compliance
- Testing required before delivery (accessibility audit)
- Cost impact: 10-20% additional development time
- Non-compliance = government can reject deliverables

### FAR Clause Flowdown

**What it is**: Clauses in your contract with the government must be flowed down to subcontractors

**Examples of clauses that flow down**:
- Cybersecurity requirements (NIST 800-171, CMMC)
- Classified information handling
- Export control compliance
- Ethics/conflicts of interest
- Environmental compliance
- Labor standards

**Implications**:
- You are responsible for subcontractor compliance
- Subcontractor non-compliance = you breach the prime contract
- Must include contractual language requiring subcontractor flow-down
- Budget for subcontractor compliance costs (CMMC, security, etc.)

## Government Procurement Terminology

| Term | Definition | Impact on Proposals |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Offeror** | Any company submitting a proposal | You are the offeror until contract award |
| **Contractor** | Company that wins the contract | You become contractor after award |
| **Subcontractor** | Company hired by prime contractor to perform work | You manage subcontractor compliance |
| **Proposal** | Formal response to RFP (also called bid, offer, submission) | Must follow RFP format exactly |
| **RFP** | Request for Proposal (solicitation for competitive bids) | Defines requirements, evaluation criteria, deadline |
| **Solicitation** | General term for any request for bids (RFP, RFQ, IFB) | Same as RFP in most contexts |
| **Statement of Work (SOW)** | Detailed description of work to be performed | Defines scope; you must address every SOW requirement |
| **Specifications** | How to build/deliver something (detailed technical requirements) | You must meet specs or proposal rejected |
| **Performance Requirements** | What deliverable must do (functional requirements) | You must demonstrate how you'll meet requirements |
| **Mandatory Requirements** | Requirements you must meet or proposal is rejected | Must address 100% of mandatory requirements |
| **Desirable Requirements** | Nice-to-have; scored but not mandatory | Differentiator; score higher if you address desirables |
| **Evaluation Criteria** | How government will score proposals | Match your response directly to evaluation criteria |
| **Best Value** | Award to best overall value (quality + cost balance) | Balance technical quality against cost |
| **LPTA** | Lowest Price, Technically Acceptable; lowest cost wins if compliant | Minimize cost; bid as low as possible |
| **Past Performance** | Your track record on similar work | References are critical; choose carefully |
| **Responsive** | Proposal meets all RFP requirements and instructions | Follow all format, page limit, submission format rules |
| **Responsible** | Company can do the work (financial stability, capacity, integrity) | Government may request financial/capacity documentation |
| **Competitive Range** | Proposals deemed competitive enough for award consideration | Top-rated proposals go into competitive range |
| **Discussions** | Negotiation with government after initial proposal evaluation | Government may ask you to revise/improve proposal |
| **Final Proposal Revision (FPR)** | Last chance to revise proposal after discussions | Usually triggered by government request |
| **Award Letter** | Official notice that you won the contract | Contract is binding; work begins |
| **Protest** | Challenge to award decision (filed with GAO) | You can protest if evaluation was unfair; costs time/money |
| **Debarment** | Prohibition from bidding on federal contracts (fraud, ethics violations) | Debarred companies cannot submit proposals |
| **Suspension** | Temporary prohibition pending investigation | May become debarment; blocks new proposals |
| **Cost Accounting Standards (CAS)** | Rules for tracking and allocating contractor costs | Compliance required if contract >$2M; audited by DCAA |
| **Facilities Capital Cost of Money (FCCM)** | Allowable cost for use of facilities/equipment | Factor into cost proposal if you own facilities |
| **Overhead/Indirect Costs** | Costs not directly traceable to specific contract (facility, HR, finance) | Audited by DCAA; must be reasonable/allocable |
| **General & Administrative (G&A)** | Corporate overhead (executive, finance, legal) | Allocated across all contracts |
| **Progress Payments** | Government pays as you perform, not after delivery | Improves cash flow; reduces contractor financing burden |
| **Contract Price Adjustment (CPA)** | Price adjustment based on inflation, labor cost changes | Only on contracts with economic price adjustment clause |
| **Termination for Convenience** | Government can cancel contract anytime, pay you for work done | Risk to contractor; limit exposure by negotiating termination costs |
| **Termination for Cause** | Government cancels due to contractor default (non-performance) | Grounds for debarment; avoid at all costs |
| **Claim** | Formal demand for payment (you believe government owes you money) | Dispute resolution process; can be costly |
| **Change Order** | Formal modification to contract scope, cost, schedule | Required if work changes; negotiate promptly |
| **Work Instruction** | Government's written direction on how to do specific work | Must follow or risk non-conformance finding |
| **Compliance Finding** | Government auditor says you violated a requirement | Must provide corrective action plan |
| **Audit** | Government review of your cost records, processes, compliance | DCAA (federal), DCIS (DoD criminal investigation) |
| **Defective Pricing** | You overcharged government due to cost estimate errors | Can result in price reduction, debarment |
| **Allowable Costs** | Costs government will reimburse (vs. unallowable costs) | Alcohol, entertainment, lobbying are unallowable |

## Key Takeaways for RFP Responders

1. **Regulation is not negotiable**: FAR/DFARS are law; compliance is mandatory, not optional
2. **Past performance matters enormously**: Government will call your references; choose wisely
3. **Set-asides matter**: If the RFP is a small business set-aside and you're large, you cannot bid
4. **Compliance matrix is essential**: Every requirement must map to a proposal section
5. **Security/CMMC delays are real**: If clearances or CMMC are required, start early
6. **Evaluation criteria drive strategy**: Write your proposal to the evaluation criteria, not to the RFP requirements
7. **Cost is only one factor**: Under best value, technical quality and past performance can trump price
8. **Subcontractor compliance is your responsibility**: Their failures are your failures
9. **Protest risk is real**: Losing a large contract can lead to GAO protests that delay award
10. **Cost accounting is audited**: DCAA will examine your labor rates, overhead allocation, cost accounting
